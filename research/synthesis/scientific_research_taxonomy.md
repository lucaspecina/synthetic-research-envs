# SREG Research Taxonomy & Classification Framework

## Purpose of this document

This document defines a taxonomy for classifying scientific research. It serves two purposes:

1. **Classifying real papers**: When analyzing a real scientific paper, use this framework to characterize what type of research it represents.
2. **Classifying synthetic research environments (SRCs)**: When analyzing a synthetic research environment generated by SREG, use this framework to characterize what type of scientific problem the agent is solving.

The goal is to have a common vocabulary that allows comparing real science with the synthetic science SREG generates — ensuring that SREG's environments cover the full breadth of scientific practice and not just a narrow slice.

---

## How to use this framework

For each research artifact (paper or SRC), you must:

1. **Assign an Objective** — what the research is trying to achieve
2. **Score each Axis** — where the research sits on each dimension, using a 0–10 score per category value

### Scoring logic

Each axis has 2–4 category values. For each value, assign a score from 0 to 10:
- **0** = this category plays no role in this research
- **1–3** = minor presence
- **4–6** = moderate presence, one of several components
- **7–9** = dominant but not exclusive
- **10** = defines the research almost entirely

Scores within an axis do **not** need to sum to 10. A research project can score 8 on data-driven and 6 on theory-driven if both are genuinely present. The scores are independent assessments, not a budget.

---

## Part 1: Research Objectives

Every research artifact has at least one objective. Objectives describe **what the research is trying to achieve**. They can be final (the point of the whole project) or intermediate (a step toward another objective).

Objectives are divided into two classes based on the nature of their output:

### Class A: Produces Knowledge (epistemic output)

These objectives succeed when they produce a true, falsifiable claim about the world.

---

#### Descriptive / Measurement

Establishes what exists and how it behaves, without explaining why. The goal is to measure well, with sufficient coverage and reported uncertainty. No hypothesis in the classical sense — the map itself is the contribution.

**Examples:**
- A consortium sequences hundreds of thousands of individual cells from human tissues, groups them by gene expression similarity, and publishes a reference map of what cell types exist in the body — without trying to explain why they exist or how they form.
- A network of sampling stations deployed across all oceans for years counts and weighs plastic fragments at each point, producing a global estimate of how much plastic floats and where it concentrates.

**Translated to questions:** What types of X exist in Y? How much is there and how is it distributed spatially? How does this change over time without my intervention?

**Identifying signal:** Many graphs and tables, obsessive focus on measuring well and reporting uncertainty. The word "cause" does not appear. The contribution is the map itself.

**Subtypes:**
- *Detection/existence*: simply establishing that something is there
- *Quantification and distribution*: how much and how it varies
- *Typology and classification*: what categories emerge from data
- *Dynamic description*: how something evolves over time without intervention

---

#### Explanatory / Causal

Establishes why something happens — from showing that an intervention works (without knowing why) to reconstructing the full molecular chain. The criterion of success is not "the model predicts well" but "the causal claim resists attempts at refutation."

**Examples:**
- Researchers randomize thousands of hospitalized COVID patients to receive dexamethasone or placebo, measure 28-day mortality, and find significant reduction in those needing oxygen or ventilation — establishing causal effect without yet knowing the molecular mechanism.
- A team installs CO₂ enrichment rings around forest plots for years, compares growth with control plots, and discovers the positive CO₂ effect disappears when soil nitrogen is depleted — identifying nitrogen as the limiting mechanism.

**Translated to questions:** Does changing A really make B change, or do they just co-occur? Through what intermediate variable does that happen? What would happen if I blocked that intermediate step?

**Identifying signal:** The question "but what if this is caused by something else?" always appears, and the paper attacks it with controls, comparison groups, or causal inference methods.

**Subtypes:**
- *Controlled association*: adjusting for confounders without intervening
- *Causal effect of intervention*: randomizing the cause and measuring the effect
- *Proximal mechanism*: identifying the first intermediate variable
- *Full molecular mechanism*: reconstructing the causal chain to its most basic level
- *Generative principle*: explaining why that mechanism exists in more general terms

---

#### Methodological

Invents a better way to look at the world. The output is an epistemic tool: a statistical method, an experimental protocol, a reporting standard. Its impact is measured by how much it improves the validity or efficiency of research others do with that tool.

**Examples:**
- Two statisticians propose controlling the expected proportion of false discoveries rather than the per-hypothesis error, demonstrate it mathematically, and illustrate it with real data — solving the problem that Bonferroni discards too many real findings when testing thousands of hypotheses.
- A group shows that ML models in medicine are evaluated with splits that allow temporal data leakage, proposes a strict protocol with temporal splits and validation in different hospitals, and shows that many previous results were inflated.

**Translated to questions:** Does this method produce more valid results than the current method? Do its theoretical properties hold when assumptions are imperfect? Does adopting this standard change what conclusions can be drawn?

**Identifying signal:** The contribution is not "I discovered X in the world" but "this is how you should do or evaluate your research." There is explicit comparison against the previous method.

**Subtypes:**
- *Domain-specific protocol*: better way to do something specific to a field
- *Statistical/computational method*: technique applicable to any field with the right data type
- *Evaluation framework and standards*: how a class of studies should be evaluated or reported
- *New experimental paradigm*: fundamentally new way to ask questions

---

#### Theoretical

Establishes general principles applicable to entire families of phenomena. The output can be a formal theorem, an abstract mathematical model, or a conceptual framework that generates broad qualitative predictions. Validation is internal coherence, predictive power over unanticipated cases, and ability to unify apparently separate phenomena.

**Examples:**
- Two researchers prove mathematically that if you average the performance of any optimization algorithm over all possible problems, no algorithm can be better than another on average — implying that any "best optimizer" claim only makes sense relative to a specific class of problems.
- A team formalizes how many training examples any learning algorithm needs to generalize, as a function of the complexity of the hypothesis class it is searching — regardless of which specific algorithm you use.

**Translated to questions:** Do these principles apply to all systems of this class or are there important exceptions? Does the framework correctly predict cases not used to build it? Does it unify phenomena that previously needed separate explanations?

**Identifying signal:** Fewer messy experiments, more general argumentation with derivations. When experiments appear, they illustrate a limiting case, not establish the main result.

**Subtypes:**
- *Formal model of specific phenomenon*: formalizing mathematically how something concrete works
- *Theory of a class of phenomena*: principles applicable to a broad family
- *Unifying framework*: showing that different things are instances of the same principle
- *Revision of foundations*: questioning or reformulating the most basic assumptions of the field

---

#### Synthesis

Does not generate primary data — integrates what others have already produced to establish what we know according to accumulated evidence. The value is not discovering something new but making an otherwise unnavigable knowledge space accessible and coherent.

**Examples:**
- A team compiles all randomized trials on exercise and depression, applies predefined inclusion criteria, and produces an estimate of the average effect with confidence intervals — resolving that yes, there is an effect but it is moderate and varies by initial severity.
- A consortium integrates data from hundreds of heterogeneous sources to produce disease burden estimates by cause, country, and year, filling gaps with models where data is missing.

**Translated to questions:** What do we know about X according to all available evidence? Is the average effect positive, negative, or null when we integrate all studies? Why do individual studies contradict each other?

**Identifying signal:** The paper has no section of own primary data collected. There is a study selection flowchart or explicit search protocol.

**Subtypes:**
- *Narrative review*: organizing and summarizing with more or less explicit criteria
- *Systematic review*: predefined reproducible protocol for identifying and evaluating evidence
- *Meta-analysis*: quantifying the average effect with uncertainty
- *With heterogeneity modeling*: not just the average but why the effect varies

---

#### Replication

Tests whether a specific finding from another group holds when someone else tries to reproduce it. Not synthesis — it is a new experiment with a very specific target. A replication failure can mean the original finding was false, there is context dependence, or your protocol differed in something important.

**Examples:**
- A consortium of 270 researchers attempts to replicate 100 social psychology studies following original protocols faithfully, and finds fewer than half produce the same result — triggering a massive revision of the field.
- An independent laboratory tries to reproduce a Nature paper reporting room-temperature superconductivity, uses the same material and protocol, and does not find the effect — initiating investigation into whether it was a measurement artifact.

**Translated to questions:** Does finding X hold when we try to reproduce it with the same protocol? Does it hold with a different design but the same hypothesis? Under what conditions does it hold and under what conditions does it not?

**Identifying signal:** The paper explicitly references a previous study it is trying to reproduce. The central question is about robustness of the finding, not about the phenomenon itself.

**Subtypes:**
- *Direct*: same protocol, same population
- *Conceptual*: same hypothesis, different design
- *With extension*: replicate and add new conditions

---

#### Benchmarking / Comparative Evaluation

Establishes which existing methods work best under which conditions, without proposing any new ones. Not the baselines section of a paper — here comparison is the main objective, with predefined criteria and no bias toward any competitor.

**Examples:**
- A group designs a battery of natural language understanding tasks, defines metrics for each, and systematically evaluates all available models publishing an updatable ranking — without proposing any model of their own.
- Researchers compile time series datasets from different domains and compare dozens of forecasting methods, finding classical simple methods outperform complex ones on short series while the opposite holds for long series.

**Translated to questions:** Which method works best for problem type X under conditions Y? Do performance differences hold when we change the dataset? Where does the best current method fail?

**Identifying signal:** The paper proposes no new model. The comparative table is the central result. Evaluation criteria are predefined before running the experiments.

**Subtypes:**
- *Ad hoc comparison*: few methods in a specific context
- *Systematic benchmark*: many methods, predefined metrics, reproducible protocol
- *With failure mode analysis*: not just who wins but where and why they fail
- *Evolutionary benchmark*: designed not to saturate as models improve

---

#### Space Exploration

Maps a space of possibilities — what options exist, which are promising, what is the internal structure of the space. Unlike descriptive research, the space being explored is partly your own construction: you defined the dimensions, properties of interest, and criterion of "promising."

**Examples:**
- A team trains a model to predict thermodynamic stability of crystal structures and uses it to evaluate millions of hypothetical crystals. The result is a map of which regions of the materials space have stable candidates and which are most promising for experimental synthesis.
- Researchers define a space of neural network architectures varying layers, sizes, and connections, systematically evaluate thousands of combinations, and produce a map of what configurations work well and why.

**Translated to questions:** What fraction of space X satisfies property Y? Where are the most promising regions? Does the space have structure we can exploit to search more efficiently?

**Identifying signal:** The output is "we found N candidates with these properties" or "the space has this structure." Not "phenomenon X works this way."

**Subtypes:**
- *Random sampling*: undirected exploration to get a general picture
- *Property-directed*: specifically searching regions satisfying defined criteria
- *Structure mapping*: understanding how the space is organized
- *Latent structure discovery*: finding unanticipated dimensions of variation

---

#### Historical / Narrative

Reconstructs how we arrived at the current state, not generalizable mechanisms. The output is a contingent causal story. Operates in evolutionary biology, geology, cosmology, history of science. You cannot replicate the event — you work with traces, indirect evidence, and narrative consistency.

**Examples:**
- Paleontologists reconstruct the T. rex extinction from fossil records, rock isotopes, and asteroid impact models — without being able to experiment, only building the narrative most consistent with all available evidence.
- Science historians reconstruct why the quantum revolution happened in central Europe in the 1920s and not at another time or place.

**Translated to questions:** How did we get from the previous state to the current one? What sequence of events caused this specific outcome? Why this path and not another?

**Identifying signal:** No experiment is possible. Validation is narrative coherence with all available evidence. Works with traces of the past: fossils, isotopes, documents, records.

---

### Class B: Produces Capabilities (functional output)

These objectives succeed when the resulting system works — predicts well, synthesizes correctly, operates autonomously — regardless of whether anyone understands why it works.

---

#### System Design / Engineering

The goal is not to understand the world but to build something that works in it. The output is an artifact whose value is measured in functional terms: speed, precision, cost, robustness, scalability. The scientific knowledge it produces is implicit in the design, not articulated as claims about the world.

**Examples:**
- A laboratory integrates a robotic arm, a crystal structure database, and an active learning algorithm that decides what synthesis to attempt next. The system tries to synthesize new materials autonomously and the paper reports how many it achieved per week compared to the human pace.
- A team builds a pipeline that takes protein sequences as input and produces 3D structure predictions in seconds — what previously required years of experimental crystallography work.

**Translated to questions:** Can this system do X under conditions Y with sufficient speed and precision to be useful? Does it keep working when it scales to larger cases or conditions change?

**Identifying signal:** The paper talks about architecture, pipeline, latency, throughput, and end-to-end tests. The "result" is that the system works under specified conditions.

**Subtypes:**
- *Assisted tool*: amplifies the human but the human decides
- *Automated pipeline*: executes predefined sequence without intervention
- *Adaptive system with closed loop*: adjusts decisions based on intermediate results
- *System with open discovery*: can redefine its own search space (does not yet exist robustly — this is SREG's long-term horizon)

---

#### Pure Predictive

Builds models whose only criterion of success is predicting well on data not seen during training. No mechanism section — error metrics, calibration, and generalization. For an early warning system, this is enough. For designing an intervention in a new system, probably not.

**Examples:**
- A team trains a neural network on decades of sales records of thousands of related products, and the model learns to produce probability distributions for future demand — outperforming classical methods without being told anything about seasonality or trends.
- Researchers compile historical radar rainfall data and landslide records, fit intensity and duration thresholds that maximize detection with minimum false positives, and validate the system on subsequent events.

**Translated to questions:** Can this model anticipate Y from X with error less than Z on data it never saw? Does the prediction hold when conditions change? Does the model know when it doesn't know?

**Identifying signal:** There is a train/test split section, out-of-sample error metrics, and comparison against baselines. Model interpretability is not a priority.

**Subtypes:**
- *Interpolation*: predicting within the already observed range
- *Extrapolation*: predicting outside the training range
- *Cross-domain*: model trained in one context applied to another
- *Rare events*: predicting the tail of the distribution where there is almost no data

---

### Support Tasks (never a final objective)

These are tasks that enable research to happen but will never be the result of any project. If someone asks "what did you discover?", the answer will never be any of these.

- **Search for information** — papers, books, patents, public data
- **Formulate the question/objective** — arriving at a precise question
- **Define variables and measurements** — operational definitions of what X is and how it is measured
- **Choose success criterion** — defining metrics, thresholds, trade-offs
- **Generate hypotheses** — proposing candidate explanations (candidates to test, not validated knowledge)
- **Propose mechanism/mental model** — building the DAG, equations, conceptual model
- **Derive observable predictions** — "if this is true, we should see X"
- **Design the study/experiment** — controls, confounders, sampling *(exception: if the design itself is the contribution, it enters methodological)*
- **Plan resources** — hardware, budget, timeline
- **Collect data / run experiments** — physical or computational execution
- **Build/implement system** — code, pipeline, instrumentation *(exception: if the system is the design/engineering objective)*
- **Run tests** — batch runs, ablations, stress tests
- **Clean and explore data (EDA)** — looking at outliers, detecting biases *(exception: if EDA reveals something surprising, it can become a descriptive finding)*
- **Estimate/train/adjust** — statistical fits, model training
- **Check validity** — sanity checks, sensitivity, internal replication
- **Interpret failures** — error analysis *(exception: if failures reveal something about the studied system, they can become an explanatory finding)*

## Part 2: Research Axes (Dimensions of Characterization)

These axes describe the **style and nature** of the research, independently of its objective. They are orthogonal — you score each one independently.

---

### Group 1: Where does the knowledge come from?

#### Axis 1.1 — Data-driven vs Theory-driven vs Literature-driven

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Data-driven** | Knowledge emerges from patterns in data — without prior hypothesis, without strong theoretical framework. The algorithm finds the structure. |
| **Theory-driven** | You start from principles or models and test them. The theory guides what you look for and how you interpret results. |
| **Literature-driven** | The starting point is integrating and extending what others have already produced. The contribution builds on accumulated knowledge. |

---

#### Axis 1.2 — Bottom-up vs Top-down

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Bottom-up** | You build understanding from data upward, without assuming structure. Patterns emerge from the data. |
| **Top-down** | You start with a model of the system and refine it. The structure is assumed and then fitted or tested. |

---

#### Axis 1.3 — Empirical vs Formal

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Empirical** | Knowledge comes from observing the world. Truth is established by evidence. |
| **Formal** | Knowledge comes from mathematical or logical derivations true by construction. Truth is established by proof. |

---

### Group 2: How is the knowledge produced?

#### Axis 2.1 — Mode of production

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Computational** | The world is studied through simulations or models. No direct contact with the real system. |
| **Experimental** | Active intervention on the real system. You change something and measure what happens. |
| **Observational** | Looking at the real system without touching it. Records of what happens naturally. |
| **Theoretical** | Formal derivation without direct data. |

---

#### Axis 2.2 — Physical context

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Wet lab** | Working with real matter — cell cultures, reagents, organisms, physical samples. |
| **Dry lab** | Computer — data analysis, simulations, models, code. |
| **Field** | The system in its natural context — ecology, geology, astronomy, social science in situ. |

---

#### Axis 2.3 — Human in the loop vs Automated

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Human in the loop** | Everything passes through human judgment. The researcher decides each step, interprets results, and chooses what to do next. |
| **Automated** | The system decides what experiment to run, executes it, analyzes the result, and decides the next step without consulting anyone. Self-driving labs are the extreme case. |

---

#### Axis 2.4 — Type of evidence

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Controlled experimental** | Someone actively intervened — randomized assignment, manipulated a variable, ran the system under controlled conditions. Allows direct causal inference. |
| **Observational** | Records of what happened naturally without intervention. Causal inference requires strong additional assumptions, rarely verifiable. |
| **Simulated** | Comes from a computational model of the system. Infinitely cheaper and more controllable, but you can only find what the simulator already knows. |

---

### Group 3: What does the research assume about the system it studies?

#### Axis 3.1 — Explicit-ontology vs Implicit-ontology

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Explicit ontology** | The relevant variables of the system are defined in advance by the researcher. You know what to measure. The vocabulary of the world is given. |
| **Implicit ontology** | The true underlying structure is unknown. Variables must be discovered or learned from data. Part of the scientific work is finding a useful way to talk about the world. |

---

#### Axis 3.2 — Mechanistic vs Phenomenological

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Mechanistic** | The model reflects the real internal structure of the system — the causes, intermediaries, processes. Allows reasoning about interventions. |
| **Phenomenological** | The model describes observable behavior without pretending to represent what happens inside. A black box that works. Does not allow reasoning about unseen interventions. |

---

#### Axis 3.3 — Closed-world vs Open-world

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Closed world** | The space of possibilities is well defined — all possible variables are known, all possible hypotheses are enumerable. |
| **Open world** | The space of possibilities is potentially infinite or unknown — variables may exist that were not anticipated, hypotheses that would never occur to you. |

---

#### Axis 3.4 — System complexity

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Simple / deterministic** | Given the current state, the next is determined and you can see everything relevant. More experiments = closer to truth. |
| **Complex / stochastic** | There is irreducible variability. A floor of uncertainty that cannot be crossed no matter how much data you collect. |
| **With hidden variables** | Causes that can never be directly measured — latent factors, inaccessible intermediate mechanisms. No matter how many experiments, you cannot infer complete causal structure from observations alone. |

---

### Group 4: Relationship with existing knowledge

#### Axis 4.1 — Incremental vs Disruptive

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Incremental** | Refines, extends, or validates existing knowledge within the current paradigm. Most science is this — and that is not bad. |
| **Disruptive** | Questions the basic assumptions of the paradigm. Rare but defines the big leaps. |

---

#### Axis 4.2 — Confirmatory vs Exploratory

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Confirmatory** | Tests a pre-specified hypothesis with maximum rigor. The protocol is defined before seeing the data. |
| **Exploratory** | Generates hypotheses from data. Maps new territories. Requires strict separation from the confirmatory phase to maintain statistical validity. |

---

#### Axis 4.3 — Disciplinary vs Interdisciplinary

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Disciplinary** | Works within a field with its established methods, language, and success criteria. |
| **Interdisciplinary** | Brings methods or concepts from another field. The risk: without deep mastery of at least one field, the result is superficial in all. |

---

### Group 5: Success Criterion (how is it evaluated?)

#### Axis 5.1 — Epistemic vs Functional

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Epistemic** | Success is having learned something true about the world — a claim that resists refutation attempts. |
| **Functional** | Success is that the system does what it is supposed to do — predicts well, synthesizes the material, operates autonomously. A research artifact can be a functional success and an epistemic failure simultaneously. |

---

#### Axis 5.2 — Type of epistemic standard (only relevant if epistemic score > 0)

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Statistical** | Success if the effect exceeds a significance threshold under the null hypothesis. |
| **Predictive** | Success if the model generalizes to new data. |
| **Causal** | Success if a cause-effect relationship was correctly identified that holds under interventions. |

---

#### Axis 5.3 — Local vs Generalizable

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| **Local** | The finding holds in these specific conditions, this dataset, this population. |
| **Generalizable** | The finding holds beyond the specific conditions of the study. |

---

## Part 3: Workflows

Workflows describe the **temporal structure** of the research — how tasks are organized over time. They are orthogonal to objectives and axes.

### Iterative workflows

**Bayesian refinement** — Start with a prior, each experiment gives evidence to update it. The posterior becomes the prior for the next round. Continue until uncertainty is sufficiently low or resources run out. Most coherent epistemologically. Requires formalizing the prior.

**Automated closed-loop** — Bayesian refinement taken to the extreme where the human is out of the loop or remains only as supervisor. Algorithm maintains a model, chooses the most informative experiment, executes it, updates the model, and repeats. Self-driving labs. SREG lives here.

**Build-test-iterate (engineering)** — Build a version of the system, test it against success criteria, identify where it fails, improve it, repeat. No explicit probabilistic model — engineering intuition, error analysis, incremental improvement.

### Linear workflows

**Hypothetico-deductive (classical)** — Start with a theory, derive a specific prediction, design the experiment most capable of falsifying it, execute, update confidence. Efficient when you already have a rich conceptual framework.

**Longitudinal observational** — Follow a system or population over time without intervening, recording how it evolves naturally. The only workflow possible when intervention is ethically or practically unfeasible.

**Synthesis / meta-analytic** — Systematically integrate data others have produced. Very defined structure: exhaustive literature search, predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria, standardized data extraction, integrated analysis.

### Parallel / multi-path workflows

**Exploratory + confirmatory (two-stage)** — First phase: explore freely in one dataset to generate hypotheses. Second phase: test those hypotheses in a completely separate dataset with a predefined protocol. The separation is key.

**Multi-method / triangulation** — The same question is attacked with multiple independent methods that have different biases. If all converge on the same result, confidence is much higher. Divergence reveals something interesting about the biases of each method.

**Generation-evaluation (massive)** — Generate a large number of candidates and evaluate them in parallel against predefined criteria. Scale is the advantage. The evaluator can be miscalibrated — a serious risk.

### Two transversal axes for all workflows

**Observational vs experimental/interventional** — Observational: watching the world as it is. Experimental: actively changing something and measuring what happens. Only intervention gives direct causality.

**Exploratory vs confirmatory** — In exploratory you generate hypotheses from data. In confirmatory you test predefined hypotheses. Mixing them without data separation produces HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results are Known) — a central problem of the replication crisis.

## Part 5: Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Real paper: AlphaFold (Jumper et al., 2021, Nature)

AlphaFold is a deep learning system that predicts the 3D structure of proteins from their amino acid sequence with atomic accuracy.

```
RESEARCH OBJECTIVE(S):
  Primary: System Design / Engineering — The output is a system that predicts protein
    structures. The criterion of success is functional (prediction accuracy on CASP targets),
    not epistemic (understanding why proteins fold as they do).
  Secondary: Space Exploration — The system enables mapping the structural space of the
    proteome, though this is downstream of the main contribution.

AXES SCORES:

Group 1 — Where does the knowledge come from?
  1.1 Data-driven:        9 — Trained on PDB structures; learns representations from
                              data without explicit physical rules.
  1.1 Theory-driven:      4 — Architecture incorporates biological prior knowledge
                              (sequences determine structure; evolutionary information
                              matters). Not dominant but not absent.
  1.1 Literature-driven:  2 — Uses known structural database but does not integrate
                              prior scientific claims as primary method.
  1.2 Bottom-up:          8 — Learns representations from data without assuming
                              intermediate structure.
  1.2 Top-down:           3 — The assumption that sequence determines structure is
                              top-down; the learning is not.
  1.3 Empirical:          9 — Truth established by prediction accuracy on held-out
                              experimental structures.
  1.3 Formal:             1 — No mathematical theorems; evaluation is empirical.

Group 2 — How is the knowledge produced?
  2.1 Computational:      10 — Entirely computational training and inference.
```

## Notes for the AI agent implementing SREG

When generating synthetic research environments (SRCs), each SRC should be characterizable using this framework. The curriculum of difficulty levels (1–5) corresponds roughly to increasing complexity on these axes:

- **Level 1**: Explicit ontology, simple/deterministic system, closed world, controlled experimental evidence, causal objective → classical hypothesis-deductive science
- **Level 2**: Add stochasticity, confounders, noisy measurements
- **Level 3**: Add hidden variables, implicit ontology partially
- **Level 4**: High-dimensional implicit ontology, phenomenological models required, complex stochastic system
- **Level 5**: Open world, hidden variables, multi-scale, mixed epistemic/functional objectives

When evaluating whether an SRC adequately represents a class of real science, compare its scores on this framework against the target paper's scores. The closer the profile, the more faithful the simulation.
