# Seed: Sugary Drink Tax — Health Gains vs Economic Equity

## Type
Policy multi-outcome with equity dimension (scenario #3)

## Domain
Public health economics / health equity / policy evaluation

## Study design
A city implemented a sugary drink tax. Evaluation data covers 500 neighborhoods
over 2 years pre/post. The tax reduced consumption overall, but effects vary
dramatically by income: low-income neighborhoods reduced consumption the most
(price-elastic) but also bear the highest tax burden relative to income. The
research question is not just "does the tax work?" but "who bears the costs
and who gets the benefits?" — a distributional question.

## Variables
- Sugar drink consumption (liters/capita/month)
- Obesity prevalence (continuous)
- Dental caries rate (continuous)
- Tax revenue collected (continuous)
- Median household income (continuous)
- Price elasticity proxy (continuous)
- Retail store density (continuous)
- Substitution to diet drinks (continuous)
- Substitution to juice (continuous)
- Neighborhood poverty rate (continuous)
- Distance to nearest grocery store (continuous)

## Key findings to inspire
- Overall consumption fell 18% (the tax "works")
- BUT: low-income neighborhoods reduced 25%, high-income only 8%
- Health benefit (obesity reduction) concentrated in low-income areas
- Tax burden (% of income spent on tax) is 3x higher for poorest quintile
- Net welfare effect depends on how you weight health vs economic burden
- Substitution to juice partially offsets health gains (juice has sugar too)
- The equity question is NOT answerable from a single aggregate effect estimate

## Why this is interesting for SREG
The SQs should probe distributional effects, not just aggregate treatment
effects. "Does the tax reduce consumption more in low-income neighborhoods?"
"Is the tax regressive in economic burden?" "Do substitution effects offset
health gains?" These are multi-outcome, equity-aware questions that don't
reduce to "X causes Y positive/negative."
